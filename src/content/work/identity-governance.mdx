## The situation

The organization runs on separate Microsoft 365 tenants, roughly a hundred of them: one per chapter plus the state. Each runs on its own Microsoft nonprofit grant. Chapter administrators are volunteers who rotate with each administration, typically every two years.

Most enterprise identity design assumes continuity: the admin who set something up will be around to explain it, and turnover is an exception to handle. Here, turnover is the normal state. Every access decision has a two-year clock on it, and the person who made it will be gone.

The symptoms were predictable once you see the pattern. Administrative access held by people who had rotated out. Board members set up as guest accounts tied to personal email by an administrator who then left. New volunteers unable to get into their own chapter’s resources, with nobody remaining who knew how access had been granted. Technical skill varies widely from one chapter to the next, so the same problem lands very differently depending on who inherits it.

<Decision
  heading="Who owns a hundred tenants"
  obvious="Consolidate the chapters into one state tenant and run it centrally."
  instead="Keep each chapter on its own tenant and grant, with tiered support behind it."
>

The membership is over 10,000, and a single grant covers up to 300 licenses. Separate grants give every chapter its own allowance, so keeping them apart is to everyone’s benefit. What the chapters need isn’t consolidation. It’s backup: someone to call, documentation written for their skill level, and a path back in when a volunteer leaves without a handoff.

</Decision>

## What I’m putting in place

- **Three tiers of support.** Chapter ownership: each chapter runs its own tenant. District support: the first place a chapter turns for help. State support: escalation, standards and continuity.
- **A standing emergency access path.** A dedicated state-level administrative account in each chapter tenant, so the organization is never locked out of its own resources when a volunteer leaves without a handoff. It exists for continuity, not day-to-day use.
- **Documentation for every tier and skill level.** In an environment with guaranteed turnover, documentation isn’t a courtesy. It’s the only thing that persists, so it has to work for the least technical person who might inherit the job.
- **Invoice billing instead of credit cards.** I’m working with Microsoft to move chapter accounts from credit card billing to invoicing. A card on file has to be kept current, and that is exactly the kind of task that gets missed when a treasurer changes.

## What comes next

Separate tenants only pay off if chapters use them. Next is a campaign to show chapters what they already have: SharePoint and document storage give each chapter a reliable place to manage and archive its records, and a web presence only its members can reach.

The [shared server I retired](/work/retiring-legacy-hosting/) is the counterexample. Chapter content lived on infrastructure nobody could maintain. A managed platform the chapter owns, with support behind it, is the opposite of that.
